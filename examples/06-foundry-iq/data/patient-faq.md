# Patient Frequently Asked Questions — Blood Pressure & Heart Medications

## General Questions

### Q: What is high blood pressure?
High blood pressure (hypertension) means the force of blood against your artery walls is consistently too high. It's often called the "silent killer" because it usually has no symptoms but can lead to serious problems like heart attack, stroke, and kidney disease if untreated.

### Q: How do I know if I have high blood pressure?
The only way to know is to have it measured. A normal reading is below 120/80 mmHg. Your GP or pharmacist can check it, or you can use a validated home monitor. If your readings are consistently above 140/90, your doctor will likely recommend treatment.

### Q: Can I lower blood pressure without medication?
Yes, lifestyle changes can make a real difference:
- Reduce salt intake to less than 6g per day (about 1 teaspoon)
- Exercise regularly — aim for 150 minutes of moderate activity per week
- Maintain a healthy weight (BMI 18.5–24.9)
- Limit alcohol to 14 units per week
- Eat more fruits, vegetables, and whole grains (the DASH diet)
- Manage stress and get adequate sleep

These changes can reduce systolic BP by 5–15 mmHg. However, many people still need medication alongside lifestyle changes.

## Medication Questions

### Q: Why do I need to take blood pressure tablets every day?
Blood pressure tablets work by keeping your blood pressure controlled throughout the day. If you miss doses or stop taking them, your blood pressure will rise again — often within 24–48 hours. Taking them at the same time each day (usually morning) helps maintain steady levels.

### Q: When should I take my blood pressure medication?
Most blood pressure medications should be taken in the morning. However, your doctor may advise evening dosing in some cases (e.g., if your nighttime readings are particularly high). Follow your doctor's specific advice.

### Q: How long until my medication starts working?
Most blood pressure medications reach full effect within 2–4 weeks. Your doctor will usually check your blood pressure 4 weeks after starting a new medication. Don't stop taking it because you feel fine — high blood pressure rarely causes symptoms.

### Q: What if I forget to take a dose?
Take it as soon as you remember, unless it's nearly time for your next dose. Never take a double dose to make up for a missed one. If you frequently forget, consider using a pill organiser or setting a daily phone alarm.

### Q: Can I stop my medication if my blood pressure is normal?
No — your blood pressure is normal BECAUSE of the medication. Stopping it will cause your blood pressure to rise again. Never stop or reduce your medication without consulting your doctor.

### Q: Do blood pressure tablets have side effects?
Like all medicines, they can cause side effects, though many people have none:
- **ACE inhibitors** (ramipril, lisinopril): dry cough (10–15% of patients), dizziness
- **ARBs** (losartan, valsartan): headache, dizziness (less cough than ACE inhibitors)
- **Calcium channel blockers** (amlodipine): ankle swelling, flushing, headache
- **Diuretics** (indapamide): increased urination, muscle cramps

If side effects bother you, tell your doctor — they can usually switch to an alternative.

### Q: Can I drink alcohol while on blood pressure medication?
Moderate alcohol is generally acceptable, but excessive drinking raises blood pressure and can interact with medications. Stick to no more than 14 units per week, spread over 3+ days. Alcohol can enhance the blood pressure-lowering effect, causing dizziness.

### Q: Can I take ibuprofen for pain?
**Caution**: NSAIDs like ibuprofen can raise blood pressure and reduce the effectiveness of blood pressure medications (especially ACE inhibitors and ARBs). They can also harm your kidneys if combined with these medications. Paracetamol is a safer choice for pain relief. If you need anti-inflammatory treatment, discuss with your doctor.

## Statin & Cholesterol Questions

### Q: Why has my doctor prescribed a statin?
Statins lower your LDL ("bad") cholesterol, which reduces your risk of heart attack and stroke. Your doctor prescribed it because your cardiovascular risk is elevated — this could be due to existing heart disease, diabetes, high blood pressure, or a combination of risk factors.

### Q: Do I need to take statins forever?
In most cases, yes. Statins reduce your ongoing risk of cardiovascular events. If you stop, your cholesterol will rise again within weeks. The protection they offer is continuous — studies show benefit increases the longer you take them.

### Q: What about muscle pain with statins?
Muscle aches occur in about 5–10% of patients. If you experience new muscle pain after starting a statin, tell your doctor. Options include reducing the dose, switching to a different statin (rosuvastatin or pravastatin cause less muscle issues), or trying alternate-day dosing.

### Q: Can I eat grapefruit while on statins?
It depends on the statin. Grapefruit can increase blood levels of simvastatin and atorvastatin (potentially causing muscle problems). It does NOT interact with rosuvastatin or pravastatin. If you eat grapefruit regularly, discuss with your pharmacist.

## Emergency Information

### Q: When should I call 999 (or local emergency services)?
Call immediately if you experience:
- Blood pressure above 180/120 with symptoms (severe headache, chest pain, breathlessness, visual changes, confusion)
- Signs of stroke: sudden face drooping, arm weakness, speech difficulty (FAST test)
- Severe chest pain or tightness lasting more than 15 minutes
- Sudden severe breathlessness

### Q: What is a hypertensive crisis?
A hypertensive crisis is a blood pressure reading above 180/120 mmHg. If accompanied by symptoms (chest pain, headache, breathlessness, visual disturbance), it requires emergency medical attention. Without symptoms, contact your GP urgently within 24 hours.
